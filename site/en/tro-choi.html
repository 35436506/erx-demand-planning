Learning Demand Planning through games

Six browser-based games, each linked to a session and a chapter of the course text. No installation, no account needed.

Beer Game · The bullwhip effect

Session 1Session 820–30 min

MIT's classic beer distribution game: you run one stage of a 4-stage chain and only see the orders from the stage below. Experience the bullwhip, then play again with sell-out data shared.

Guess the Pegels cell

Session 5Session 710 min

Ten random series: read the trend line (flat · straight · curved) and the seasonal amplitude (constant · widening) to pick the right cell of the Pegels matrix and the forecasting method family.

Human vs machine · Forecasting challenge

Session 3Session 4Session 6Session 815 min / series

Forecast 12 consecutive months for real MBF series (stable, summer, trend, Tết, promotion). Your error is compared with Naive, Seasonal Naive and SES — does your judgement add value (FVA)?

ABC × XYZ challenge

Session 715 min

Classify 23 MBF items into ABC by value and XYZ by variability (beware the raw-CV trap of seasonal items), then choose a forecasting – inventory policy for each cell.

Inventory challenge (s, Q) · (R, S)

Session 915–20 min

Choose the reorder point and order quantity (or review period and order-up-to level) for 52 weeks of random demand. Reach a fill rate ≥ 98% at the lowest cost — then switch on variable lead times to see the price of uncertainty.

Play now

Newsvendor · Producing Tết goods

Session 910 min

Eight Tết gift products, each produced only once. Use the critical ratio to decide whether to make more or less than the mean forecast — and separate good decisions from lucky outcomes.

Tips for running them in an online class

Before

The instructor posts the game link in the chat; learners open it on a computer (phones work too).

During

Play in breakout rooms for 10–20 minutes. Each group screenshots its final result and pastes it into the chat.

After

Discuss the questions at the end of the game and link them to the concepts in the slides. Whoever holds the best record shares how they did it.

The Beer Game is based on MIT's beer distribution game (Sterman, 1989); the computer-run stages order with Sterman's anchoring-and-adjustment model. The other games use the Mekong Bloom Foods case data or simulated data.
